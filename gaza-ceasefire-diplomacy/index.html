Gaza at One Year: The Ceasefire That Exists in Name Only — and the Disarmament Deadlock Nobody Can Break

One year after the October 9, 2025 agreement, the US envoy celebrated milestones while the ledger of the war kept growing. This is what a peace process looks like when the shooting never quite stopped.

The signal

On October 9, 2026, Jared Kushner — President Trump's son-in-law and the administration's envoy for the Gaza file — marked the first anniversary of the agreement on Trump's 20-point Gaza plan with a public post listing what he called a year of real progress: the hostages home, a Board of Peace, a technocratic Palestinian administration, a phased disarmament framework signed in July. And then the concession that carried more weight than the celebration: there is still hard work ahead, because disarmament "must be implemented," security established, services restored, communities rebuilt.

The anniversary thus produced two competing one-year assessments that do not contradict each other so much as describe different wars. In Kushner's telling, a diplomatic architecture now exists that did not exist a year ago — institutions, frameworks, a path. In the telling of the Financial Times, as relayed by the Diplomatic Cable newsletter, the ceasefire exists in name only: more than 1,400 Israeli strikes since it took effect, roughly 1,400 Palestinians killed, an average of nearly four strikes a day. Israel says it is hitting militants who are rearming or attacking its troops. That description, the newsletter notes, is of an ongoing war, not a ceasefire. Both accounts are sourced. Both can be true. The question the anniversary forces is what a peace agreement means when its central security provision — the disarmament of Hamas — has not happened, and when the mechanism meant to enforce the peace has not deployed a single soldier.

What we can verify

The anniversary itself is VERIFIED: on October 9, 2026, Kushner published a post marking one year since the October 9, 2025 agreement, as reported by the Times of Israel, JBizNews, and others. The post's contents are VERIFIED as statements — what Kushner claimed, not necessarily what is so. He wrote that the agreement created a path to reunite hostage families, and he listed milestones: the return of the 20 living hostages to Israel in October 2025, the return of the remains of the final hostage in January 2026, the establishment of the Board of Peace and an international stabilization force, and the formation of a technocratic Palestinian administration to govern the Gaza Strip. He credited Hamas with having signed a phased disarmament framework in July, while acknowledging the process has yet to be fully implemented. His forward agenda — completing disarmament, establishing security, restoring essential services, rebuilding communities — is VERIFIED as his stated position.

The hostage accounting is STRONGLY_SUPPORTED: the last living hostages were returned in October 2025 and the remains of the last deceased hostage were recovered in January 2026, completing the return of all 251 people taken captive in the October 7, 2023 attack, according to the Board of Peace's report to the United Nations as relayed by Business Standard. Note the attribution chain: the figure comes from the Board of Peace, an interested institution, reported through the press. The broad fact of the returns is corroborated across multiple outlets; the precise institutional framing belongs to the Board.

The disarmament deadlock is VERIFIED in its structure, if not in every detail. In July 2026, President Trump announced that the Board of Peace had reached an agreement with Hamas on the group's disarmament and the transfer of civilian and security control of Gaza to a new Palestinian technocratic government (Business Standard). Hamas subsequently linked its disarmament to wider political conditions, including the establishment of a Palestinian state, while Israel continued to demand the complete dismantling of Hamas's military capabilities before a full withdrawal. Dawn's October 10, 2026 reporting adds the sequencing detail: a disarmament framework was agreed in July but stalled because Israel demands full demilitarization before withdrawal, and August talks produced no breakthrough. The sequencing dispute — complete disarmament first versus gradual, reciprocal steps — is therefore VERIFIED as the live obstacle, reported by multiple outlets in consistent terms.

The continuing violence is ATTRIBUTED but substantial: the Financial Times counts more than 1,400 Israeli strikes since the ceasefire took effect and roughly 1,400 Palestinians killed, as relayed by Ivo Daalder's Diplomatic Cable newsletter of October 10, 2026. This newsroom has not independently reviewed the FT's count, so it is carried as attributed reporting from a reputable outlet, not as a newsroom-verified tally. What is VERIFIED is the pattern it describes: the United Nations said in February 2026 that Gaza was still not at peace, with Israeli strikes and exchanges of fire continuing despite the ceasefire (Business Standard).

The institutional shortfalls are STRONGLY_SUPPORTED across the newsletter's cited references: five countries pledged troops for the stabilization force in February, but none has deployed; the Board of Peace unveiled a $2.45 billion, six-month recovery program with a first wave of 500 troops from Morocco and Kosovo, but set no start date because nearly everything hinges on disarming Hamas; donors pledged $17 billion in February, but only $280 million has arrived. These figures come with reference markers to underlying reporting that this newsroom has not independently opened; they are carried as attributed, multi-sourced where the newsletter's citations overlap, and flagged where single-sourced.

The humanitarian picture is VERIFIED in its outlines: the UN migration agency reports only 17 percent of winter shelter needs met, with Israel barring 8,600 emergency shelter kits as "dual-use" (via the Diplomatic Cable). UN Secretary-General António Guterres, in an October 5, 2026 statement ahead of the third anniversary of the October 7 attack, called for the ceasefire to become permanent and for humanitarian assistance at scale; he acknowledged more than 1,200 Israelis and foreign nationals killed and 251 taken hostage in the Hamas attack, and cited the Palestinian Health Ministry's figure of more than 74,200 dead in Gaza — figures the UN deems reliable (The Hindu, Malay Mail). Israel still controls most of Gaza and refuses to withdraw its soldiers until Hamas's disarmament has been verified; Hamas advocates gradual disarmament rather than immediate, unilateral surrender of its arsenal (The Hindu).

What is being claimed

Kushner's anniversary post advances a narrative of momentum: that the agreement ended the hostage crisis, built institutions, and put disarmament on a signed track — and that the remaining work is implementation, not negotiation. This is ANALYSIS presented as accomplishment, and it should be read as the envoy's brief for his own diplomacy. The milestones he lists are largely institutional facts (a board exists, a framework was signed); the progress they represent is interpretive. A signed disarmament framework that has not been implemented is, from the Gazan perspective, a piece of paper. From the Israeli perspective, it is a commitment Hamas is evading. From the American perspective, it is proof the process works. The same document, three readings — which is why the claim-status discipline matters here more than usual.

The counter-narrative, advanced most sharply by the Diplomatic Cable, is that the process is managed optics: talks as process, not progress. Its evidence is the strike count, the undeployed force, the undelivered pledges, the unmet shelter needs. This too is ANALYSIS, albeit evidence-dense analysis. Its weak point is the same as Kushner's strength: it underweights what the existence of any framework, however unimplemented, changed about the diplomatic geometry — the fact that Hamas signed anything at all on disarmament, that a technocratic administration exists on paper, that the hostages are home.

A third claim circulates in regional commentary: that the Iran war — the US-Israeli war on Iran now in its eighth month — is creating new incentives for a Gaza deal, because a regional settlement becomes more valuable as the regional war burns. This is ATTRIBUTED commentary, plausible as logic, unverified as causation. No official has said on the record that Iran-war pressure is moving the Gaza file, and the sequencing deadlock predates the Iran war's current phase.

What remains genuinely UNCONFIRMED: the current state of Hamas's arsenal (no independent inventory exists in public), the real content of the August talks (no readout beyond "no breakthrough"), and whether the $2.45 billion recovery program has any funding beyond pledges.

The strategic picture

To understand why disarmament is stuck, it helps to stop treating it as a technical problem and see it as what it is: the point where every party's survival logic collides. Disarmament, demobilization, and reintegration — DDR, in the practitioners' jargon — has a known playbook. It worked, after a fashion, in Northern Ireland, where the IRA's decommissioning was stretched over years and verified by an independent commission both sides could tolerate. It worked partially in Colombia, where the FARC's demobilization was tied to political participation and transitional justice. It failed or stalled wherever one side demanded surrender first and talks later — which is precisely the sequencing Israel is demanding now.

The structural problem in Gaza is that disarmament is being asked to do three jobs at once, and they pull in different directions. First, it is Israel's security guarantee: no withdrawal without verified demilitarization, because every previous withdrawal — Lebanon in 2000, Gaza in 2005 — was followed, in Israel's reading, by the rearmament of its enemies. This is not paranoia as rhetoric; it is the operative lesson of Israeli strategic culture, and any analysis that dismisses it will misread every Israeli move. Second, disarmament is Hamas's sole bargaining chip: the one asset that makes it a party to negotiations rather than a spent force to be policed. To surrender it unilaterally, before any political horizon, is to negotiate the terms of one's own irrelevance. Third, disarmament is the international community's precondition for money: no donor will fund reconstruction on top of an armed Hamas, and no stabilization force will deploy into an active weapons zone. So the money waits on the force, the force waits on disarmament, disarmament waits on politics, and politics waits on the war ending — which it hasn't, quite.

This is the trap the July framework walked into. A phased, reciprocal framework — disarm in stages as Israel withdraws in stages — is the only design that has ever worked anywhere, because it lets each side test the other's compliance before taking the next irreversible step. Hamas accepted exactly this logic. Netanyahu rejected it, insisting on complete disarmament before any withdrawal. Put plainly: Israel is demanding the Northern Ireland outcome on a surrender timeline, and Hamas is offering the Colombia timeline while keeping the Northern Ireland ambiguity about finality. There is no overlap between those positions, which is why August produced no breakthrough and why Kushner's "must be implemented" reads less like a plan than a wish.

The regional layer makes the trap deeper, not shallower. The standard optimistic argument — that the Iran war creates incentives for a Gaza settlement — deserves scrutiny. It is true that a Gaza deal would let Washington and its regional partners claim a diplomatic win while the Iran war grinds on, and true that Saudi normalization logic has always linked Gaza quiet to broader bargains. But the Iran war also does the opposite: it consumes American diplomatic bandwidth, it makes Israel's security establishment less willing to take risks on a second front's arrangements, and it gives Hamas's remaining hardliners a reason to believe that time favors them — that a region in flames is not a region where anyone will force their disarmament. The war is simultaneously an incentive and an excuse, which means it predicts nothing. ANALYSIS, stated plainly: anyone who tells you the Iran war's effect on Gaza diplomacy is straightforward is selling something.

Then there is the money, which is where the process meets reality most brutally. The arithmetic of the anniversary is devastating: $17 billion pledged in February, $280 million arrived. A $2.45 billion six-month recovery program unveiled with no start date. A UN/EU/World Bank reconstruction estimate of roughly $70 billion against a Gazan economy that has been flattened. Donor conferences are a well-studied ritual in this conflict — pledges are announced, disbursements lag, and the gap becomes its own political fact. The reasons are not mysterious: donors will not build what the next round of fighting will destroy, treasuries have their own politics, and the disarmament precondition gives everyone a respectable reason to wait. But the effect is that Gaza's 2.1 million people, most in tents or displacement sites prone to flooding as winter approaches, are living inside a peace process whose economic content is, so far, hypothetical. Only 17 percent of winter shelter needs met is not a statistic about logistics. It is a verdict on the process.

The competing narratives

Narrative one — the administration's: the 20-point plan worked. The hostages are home — all 251 accounted for, the living returned in October 2025, the last remains in January 2026. A Board of Peace exists where nothing existed. A technocratic administration exists on paper. Hamas signed a disarmament framework, a thing no one thought possible. The remaining work is implementation, and implementation is hard, but the direction of travel is set. In this telling, the anniversary is a progress report, and "hard work ahead" is candor, not confession. The evidence for this narrative is institutional and real: the things Kushner lists do exist. Its weakness is the gap between existence and function — a stabilization force with no deployed troops, a recovery program with no start date, a disarmament framework with no disarmament.

Narrative two — the skeptics': the ceasefire is a fiction maintained for diplomatic convenience. The Financial Times' strike count is the sharpest version: 1,400 strikes, 1,400 dead, four strikes a day. Add the undeployed force, the undelivered billions, the barred shelter kits, and the picture is of a war that changed form — from bombardment campaign to a grinding, low-visibility counterinsurgency under a diplomatic label. In this telling, the anniversary post is reputation management, and the "thoughtful plan" is a PowerPoint. The evidence for this narrative is material and accumulating. Its weakness is that it cannot easily account for the hostages' return — the single largest humanitarian deliverable, which did happen — or for why Hamas would sign a disarmament framework at all if the process were pure theater.

Narrative three — the structuralists': both of the above are true because they describe different levels. At the diplomatic level, real architecture was built; at the ground level, the war's logic was never interrupted. This is the reading this newsroom finds most consistent with the evidence, and it is the least satisfying, because it offers no clean verdict. It says: the 20-point plan was a genuine diplomatic achievement that solved the hostage crisis and created institutions, AND the ceasefire has not held in any meaningful sense for Gazans, AND disarmament is blocked by a sequencing dispute that reflects real, rational fears on both sides. The anniversary, in this reading, is not a progress report or a fraud — it is a photograph of a process that succeeded at what diplomacy can do (deals, frameworks, returns) and failed at what only politics can do (answer the question of who governs Gaza and with what guns).

There is a fourth narrative, quieter, mostly in Israeli domestic discourse: that the current arrangement suits the government — a "ceasefire" that permits continued strikes, a disarmament demand designed to be unmeetable, a process that runs without forcing the hard choices about Gaza's future. This is ALLEGED as motive, not verified as fact; motives are the hardest thing to source. But it is worth stating because it explains the otherwise puzzling shape of Israeli policy: maximalist demands plus continued military freedom plus no political horizon equals a status quo that is, for now, domestically costless. If that is the strategy, the August "no breakthrough" was not a failure but the product working as designed. This newsroom marks that as interpretation, not evidence — but interpretation with explanatory power.

The missing piece

What is missing from every anniversary account is the one document that would settle the largest factual disputes: an independent, public accounting of the disarmament framework itself. What did Hamas actually sign in July? What are the phases, the timelines, the verification mechanisms, the reciprocal Israeli steps? Kushner says a framework was signed; Trump announced an agreement; Dawn reports it stalled on sequencing. But the text — if a text exists in any complete form — has not been published. Without it, every claim about who is blocking what rests on secondhand characterizations by interested parties. This is the single most important transparency gap in the file, and it is remarkable how little attention it receives: the central security bargain of the Gaza endgame is being debated entirely through leaks and posts.

The second missing piece is any credible Palestinian voice in the anniversary narrative. Kushner's post thanks a long list — Trump, Witkoff, the vice president, Rubio, Egypt, the UAE, Qatar, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Israel. The technocratic administration is mentioned as an institution. But the 2.1 million Gazans living the process appear in the anniversary only as beneficiaries of future rebuilding. Their representatives, their conditions, their consent to the disarmament sequencing — absent. A peace process that does not include the population whose disarmament is at issue is not a peace process; it is an administration plan. This is not sentiment. It is the lesson of every DDR effort ever attempted: disarmament without the buy-in of the armed group's constituency fails, and buy-in requires a political horizon the current framework does not offer.

The third missing piece is verification itself. Who verifies disarmament, with what access, and what happens when verification is disputed? The Board of Peace was meant to carry this, but a board that has deployed no troops cannot verify anything. The history here is instructive: the IRA decommissioned under General John de Chastelain, a figure both sides accepted; Colombia under UN monitors with a mandate both sides negotiated. Gaza has neither a verifier nor a mandate — just a demand (Israel's) and a framework (unsigned in public, unimplemented in practice). Until that gap closes, "disarmament must be implemented" is a sentence in search of a mechanism.

What changed

The anniversary itself is the change under examination: one year on, the file moved from negotiation to implementation — and implementation is where it stalled. Three specific developments define the current state. First, the July disarmament framework converted the disarmament question from "whether" to "how," which is genuine progress of a kind: Hamas put its name to phased disarmament, a concession with no precedent in the group's history. But the framework's non-publication and the immediate sequencing fight mean the "how" is now the entire conflict, relocated rather than resolved. Second, the institutional architecture thickened without activating: the Board of Peace produced a $2.45 billion blueprint, the technocratic administration was announced, troop pledges were collected — and none of it is operating. The gap between announced and actual is now the story, where a year ago the story was whether anything would be announced at all. Third, the humanitarian clock ran out the diplomacy: with winter approaching and shelter needs at 17 percent, the process is now racing a seasonal deadline it did not create and cannot pause. A year ago the question was whether a deal could be signed. Today it is whether a signed deal can survive its first winter.

What to watch

Watch the disarmament text. If the July framework is ever published — or substantively leaked — read the sequencing provisions first and the verification provisions second. Everything else is commentary. Watch the troop deployments: the first 500 (Morocco, Kosovo) are the tripwire. If they deploy, the process has a security backbone; if the start date keeps slipping, the Board of Peace is a letterhead. Watch the money actually moving: the $280 million disbursed against $17 billion pledged is the number that matters, updated monthly. Watch the strike count: whether the Financial Times' tally keeps climbing at four a day or plateaus will tell you, better than any statement, whether the "ceasefire" is stabilizing or fiction. Watch Guterres's permanent-ceasefire call — whether it gathers any state backing or dies as ritual language. And watch the Israeli election calendar: the October 7 commemorations coincided with preparations for a general election, and no Israeli government in campaign mode will spend political capital on Gaza concessions. The disarmament deadlock will not break before the politics allow it to, and the politics are not allowing it yet.

General License 135: The Year-Plus Carve-Out That Rewrote the Sanctions Map

Buried in a Treasury notice issued minutes after the Trump-Putin call: Russian diesel authorized through April 7, 2027. The word "temporary" is doing more work than any word in sanctions policy this year.

The signal

On October 9, 2026, minutes after Donald Trump announced his diesel arrangement with Vladimir Putin, the US Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control published General License No. 135. The document authorizes, through 12:01 in the morning Eastern time on April 7, 2027, all transactions related to the sale, delivery, unloading, and importation — including importation into the United States — of diesel fuel of Russian Federation origin that would otherwise be prohibited under the Russian sanctions regulations. The license sets no cap on volumes. Its only stated exclusion preserves the blocking of accounts held at US financial institutions by Russia's central bank, its National Wealth Fund, and its Finance Ministry.

Read that date again: April 7, 2027. The midterm elections are November 3, 2026 — five months before the license expires. What was presented as emergency relief for fuel prices ahead of an election is, on the face of the legal instrument, a year-and-a-half sanctions regime for Russian refined products. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy called it an obvious sign of weakness that plays into Russia's hands. Former president Petro Poroshenko, speaking at a security conference in Sweden, did the arithmetic out loud: the world consumes four million tons of diesel a day, and 300,000 tons a month is, in his word, zero — a symbolic message, not a market intervention. The European Union, for its part, will not follow: Brussels is preparing to add 827 entities and 744 individuals to its sanctions lists instead.

This paper dissects the license text, reconstructs the political and market context that produced it, evaluates the competing claims about what it will achieve, and examines what a year-plus carve-out means for the architecture of Western sanctions — an architecture built, since 2022, on the proposition that Russian energy revenues and Western markets must be separated, now perforated by the very government that built it.

What we can verify

The license text. General License No. 135 was published by OFAC on October 9, 2026. Its operative language authorizes transactions involving the sale, delivery, unloading, and import of diesel fuel of Russian origin, including imports into the United States, through 12:01am EDT on April 7, 2027. Transactions in Russian diesel had been prohibited under sanctions imposed in early 2022. The license does not authorize debits from accounts at US financial institutions owned by the Central Bank of the Russian Federation, Russia's National Wealth Fund, or the Russian Finance Ministry. The license sets no volumetric cap. These facts are VERIFIED — the text is published, and its contents are reported consistently by the New Voice of Ukraine, Interfax-Ukraine, The Week, SCMP, LIGA.net, and the Ukrainian outlet Ukrmedia.

The timing. The license was issued on October 9, the same day Trump announced the Putin arrangement, and according to The Hindu's reporting, minutes after the Trump-Putin call. The sequencing — presidential announcement, then immediate legal instrument — indicates the arrangement was pre-negotiated, not improvised. VERIFIED as reported chronology.

The volumes. Three figures circulate, and they describe different things. Trump initially spoke of 300,000-plus metric tons to be supplied immediately. Ukrainian outlet LIGA.net reported 4.8 million metric tons to be shipped in the near future in several phases. The Week reported that Russia will release diesel in tranches, with volumes tied to refinery conditions and rising to as much as 3,000,000 tonnes per month. These are not contradictory — they describe an initial tranche, a near-term program, and a ramp-up ceiling respectively — but they come from different sources of varying authority (presidential statement, Ukrainian press, Indian press citing Russian plans). Status: REPORTED, with the tranche structure ATTRIBUTED to Russian planning as relayed by press.

The Russian side. Russian Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak said Moscow would begin lifting the diesel export ban in place since July. Putin's special envoy Kirill Dmitriev wrote on X that Russia-US cooperation on diesel and energy will benefit the world. A Kremlin website statement confirmed Russia's readiness to supply oil and petroleum products to US and global markets, expressing confidence in a positive impact on the entire global economy, and noted that the leaders devoted considerable attention to prospects for resolving the war against Ukraine. These are VERIFIED statements by Russian officials — they establish Moscow's public position, not the underlying facts of supply.

The Ukrainian reaction. Zelenskyy said on X that any easing of sanctions against Russia without a clear and lasting de-escalation agreement is an obvious sign of weakness, playing into Russia's hands, encouraging more killing, a longer war, less respect for the United States, and greater global damage — and that Ukraine counts on a strong American conversation with Russia, not a weak one. Poroshenko, at the Visby security conference in Sweden, argued the volumes are symbolic against four million tons of daily world consumption, warned that Putin reads concessions as weakness rather than goodwill, and urged maintained unity. Both statements are VERIFIED as made; their characterizations of the policy's effects are OPINION and ANALYSIS respectively.

The European position. The EU will not ease its sanctions on Russian diesel. The European External Action Service has proposed adding 827 legal entities and 744 individuals — predominantly Russian defense-industrial companies and associated persons — to the sanctions lists, for consideration at the Foreign Affairs Council in Luxembourg on October 12. The US authorization is described by the Ukrainian press as temporary and as not lifting all American sanctions. Status: VERIFIED as reported EU posture; the listings await the Council decision.

The American political context. An Associated Press-NORC poll found 65 percent of US adults blame Trump's policies more than external factors for persistently high costs. The midterm elections are November 3. US lawmakers from both parties have criticized the arrangement as undermining sanctions and funding the Kremlin's war economy, according to The Week. A Ukrainian delegation — including presidential office head Budanov, foreign intelligence chief Umerov, and parliamentary faction leader Arakhamia — was in Washington for negotiations on Ukraine peace around the same time. Secretary of State Rubio has said the Russia-Ukraine war has reached an impasse with no prospect of negotiated resolution. The poll figure is VERIFIED (AP-NORC); the lawmaker criticism is REPORTED via The Week; the delegation composition is REPORTED via LIGA.net.

The diesel market context. US retail diesel averaged $6.39 a gallon in early October, with the EIA expecting it above $6 through the month before easing toward roughly $4.50 a gallon on average in 2027. Ultra-low-sulfur diesel futures traded near $4.65 a gallon; New York Harbor spot was around $5. In Britain, diesel reached 200 pence per liter — near the post-2022 record — pushing a typical fill-up to almost £110, about £31 above pre-Iran-war levels. European diesel refining margins hit an all-time high near $95 a barrel on September 23, still around $78 on October 1. Asia's gasoline margin set a record after Chinese refiners suspended product exports beyond Hong Kong and Macau. US distillate inventories fell 2.3 million barrels in the latest EIA week to 105.2 million barrels, below the five-year average. Energy Secretary Chris Wright said he is highly confident Europe will draw down emergency diesel stocks. The EIA's October Short-Term Energy Outlook raised its Brent forecast to about $98 a barrel for 2026 (up 8 percent), sees $105 in the fourth quarter, and $84 in 2027. These are VERIFIED market data points via EIA, Argus, and the FT/industry press.

What is being claimed

The administration's claim: this is temporary emergency relief. The framing — a temporary license to ease fuel prices — presents the carve-out as crisis management, not policy change. The license's own text undercuts the framing: "temporary" authorization through April 2027 is eighteen months of authorized trade, covering two American electoral cycles' worth of fuel markets. A genuinely temporary emergency measure would run weeks or a few months; eighteen months is a regime. The administration's characterization is best read as political framing of a substantive policy shift.

Moscow's claim: cooperation that benefits the world. Dmitriev's and Putin's statements frame the arrangement as mutually beneficial global economic stewardship. What they omit is the leverage dimension: every renewal date is now a negotiation, and Russia's ability to modulate refinery output — volumes are explicitly tied to refinery conditions — gives Moscow a tap it can turn. The "benefit the world" framing is state messaging; the structural effect is Russian leverage over Western fuel markets.

Kyiv's claim: weakness that funds aggression. Zelenskyy's argument — easing without de-escalation rewards Moscow and lengthens the war — is a political judgment, not a factual claim, and should be evaluated as such. Its factual premise (the easing is real and unilateral) is correct. Its prediction (more resources → more aggression) is ANALYSIS grounded in the reasonable premise that revenue fungibility applies to war economies, but it is not verifiable in advance.

Poroshenko's arithmetic. The claim that 300,000 tons a month is "zero" against four million tons of daily world consumption is directionally correct arithmetic with a rhetorical edge. Three hundred thousand tons a month is roughly 10,000 tons a day — about 0.25 percent of daily world diesel consumption. Against global volumes, it is indeed marginal. But the relevant market is not global consumption — it is the Atlantic-basin diesel balance, where even modest incremental barrels can move refining margins at the edge. Poroshenko's math is correct; his conclusion (no price impact whatsoever) overstates it. At the 3-million-tonnes-per-month ramp-up figure, the volumes become genuinely material to regional balances.

The India double-standard claim. The Week's observation — that India faced American pressure and tariff threats for buying Russian crude while Washington now authorizes its own Russian fuel imports — is a factual juxtaposition, and it is accurate as far as it goes. Whether it constitutes a "double standard" depends on distinctions the administration would draw (product versus crude, licensed versus unlicensed, wartime exigency) — but the political optics in New Delhi are unambiguous, and the episode will be cited in every future American sanctions conversation with India.

The strategic picture

Start with what sanctions are. Since early 2022, the Western sanctions regime on Russian energy has rested on a simple proposition: Russian hydrocarbon revenues fund the war against Ukraine, so those revenues must be separated from Western markets. The architecture was never airtight — price caps, shadow fleets, third-country refining — but its direction was consistent: shrink Moscow's energy income, deny its products Western premium markets. General License 135 reverses the direction for a major product category, for eighteen months, by the stroke of a Treasury pen, without a public policy debate, without congressional action, and — per the available reporting — without allied consultation. That is what makes it a regime change rather than a waiver: waivers are exceptions that prove rules; this is large enough and long enough to become the rule for diesel.

The duration is doing the strategic work. Consider the incentives it creates. For Moscow, the license is a year-plus revenue channel for refined products at a moment when its refineries are under Ukrainian drone attack and its export options are constrained — plus a built-in renegotiation point in April 2027, when the license's renewal becomes leverage over whoever sits in the White House. For American fuel markets, it is eighteen months of Russian supply dependence — the exact dependence the sanctions were meant to prevent — which will create a domestic constituency (refiners, distributors, trucking) for renewal when the date approaches. For Ukraine, it is eighteen months of watching Russian diesel revenues flow while its own refineries burn. For Europe, which pointedly refuses to follow, it is a transatlantic split: American diesel markets reintegrated with Russia, European markets not, with all the arbitrage and enforcement complexity that implies.

The exclusion clause — Russian central bank, National Wealth Fund, and Finance Ministry accounts remain blocked — is the administration's answer to the charge of wholesale sanctions relief. And it is a real constraint: it means the product can flow while the sovereign money stays frozen. But the history of sanctions evasion suggests skepticism about how cleanly product flows can be separated from money flows over eighteen months. Diesel does not sell itself; it moves through traders, shippers, insurers, and banks, and each intermediary is a potential leakage point. The license's narrowness is genuine on paper; its enforceability over a year and a half of global trade is the open question.

The midterm dimension cannot be separated from the policy, because the administration has not separated them. The AP-NORC finding — 65 percent of Americans blame Trump's policies for high costs — is the political fact that makes diesel a campaign issue. The license runs five months past the election, which means its expiration will fall in the next administration's lap regardless of who wins in November — or, if Trump-aligned Republicans hold power, its renewal will be a live political question in 2027. Sanctions policy made on electoral timelines tends to produce exactly this shape: long enough to survive the news cycle, short enough to be called temporary.

There is a further irony that the India angle captures. For months, Washington pressured New Delhi over discounted Russian crude purchases, wielding tariff threats. Now Washington licenses its own Russian diesel imports through April 2027. Whatever the technical distinctions, the signal to every sanctions-targeted capital is unmistakable: American sanctions discipline is negotiable when American fuel prices are at stake. That lesson will outlive this license. The next time Washington asks a partner to absorb economic pain for sanctions solidarity, the partner will remember October 2026.

Finally, place the license inside the war economy it inhabits. Ukraine continues large-scale drone strikes on Russian refineries — the physical counterpart to the financial maneuvering. Russia's willingness to supply diesel in tranches tied to refinery conditions is itself an admission: its refining sector is damaged and constrained. The license thus arrives at a moment when Russian diesel supply is least certain — which is either clever timing (lock in the legal framework before supply recovers) or a sign that the volumes will underdeliver. Poroshenko's skepticism about volumes and Zelenskyy's alarm about revenues can both be true: the license may move fewer barrels than advertised while still establishing the precedent that matters.

The longer history: how the sanctions architecture was built

To grasp what General License 135 undoes, recall what was built. When Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, the West's economic response centered on a novel instrument: rather than embargoing Russian energy outright — which would have spiked global prices and punished Western consumers — the G7, the EU, and allies constructed a system designed to keep Russian oil flowing while shrinking Moscow's revenue. The centerpiece was the price cap: Russian crude could be shipped using Western insurance and services only if sold at or below a set price. The EU layered on its own embargo of Russian seaborne crude (December 2022) and refined products (February 2023), with the diesel ban in particular reshaping European fuel markets. The United States banned Russian oil, gas, and coal imports outright in March 2022.

The architecture was porous by design and by evasion. A shadow fleet of aging tankers with opaque ownership moved Russian crude outside Western insurance. Third countries — India and China most prominently, but also Turkey, the UAE, and others — bought discounted Russian barrels, refined some of them, and sold products onward, including, through well-documented laundering chains, back into Western markets. Enforcement was uneven and often performative. But the direction of travel was consistent for four years: tighten the cap, designate the shadow fleet, sanction the enablers, and defend the principle that Russian energy revenues must not fund the war machine at Western expense.

General License 135 is the first major Western instrument to move in the opposite direction — and it does so more cleanly than any evasion scheme. Shadow-fleet laundering at least preserved the fiction of compliance; the license abandons the fiction and authorizes the trade openly, at any volume, for eighteen months. The irony is sharp: after four years of designating shadow tankers — including 22 more on October 9, the very day the license was published — Washington has created a legal channel wider than anything the shadow fleet achieved. The sanctions architects now face the question their critics always posed: if the regime's own authors suspend it when fuel prices bite, was it ever a principled architecture, or was it always a contingent one, held together only while the economic pain was bearable?

The European refusal to follow sharpens the point. Brussels is not merely maintaining sanctions; it is expanding them, with 827 entities and 744 individuals proposed for listing. The result, if both postures hold, is a bifurcated West: American diesel markets legally open to Russian product, European markets closed, with the EU's enforcement apparatus tasked with policing a boundary its closest ally has erased. Sanctions have always depended on coalition discipline — the price cap worked, to the extent it did, because the G7 moved together. A coalition of one is not a coalition. The license's deepest damage may not be the barrels it authorizes but the precedent it sets: that Western sanctions on Russian energy are negotiable instruments of domestic politics, not commitments of foreign policy.

The competing narratives

Reading one: pragmatic crisis management. On this reading, the license is what it says it is — a temporary measure to relieve a genuine fuel emergency. American diesel at $6.39 a gallon, British diesel near record highs, European refining margins at all-time records, EIA inventories below five-year averages: the shortage is real, the midterm pressure is real, and 300,000 tons a month (ramping higher) is real supply. The eighteen-month duration gives markets the certainty they need to contract and ship; a 90-day waiver would not move a single tanker. The sovereign-account exclusions preserve the sanctions' core. On this reading, critics are confusing emergency economics with grand strategy.

Reading two: normalization by the back door. On this reading, the license is the beginning of sanctions unwind disguised as emergency relief. Eighteen months, no volume cap, issued without allied consultation days after a presidential call with Putin, against the backdrop of a Ukrainian delegation in Washington and a Secretary of State declaring the war at an impasse — the pattern suggests a policy direction, not a one-off. The "temporary" label is doing political work: it lets the administration claim continuity while practicing reversal. Once Russian diesel flows into American markets for a year and a half, the commercial and political constituencies for its continuation will make the April 2027 expiry a formality. On this reading, the license is the most significant Western sanctions reversal since 2022.

Reading three: the leveraged pause. A third reading sees the license as neither relief nor reversal but as a bargaining instrument in a larger negotiation. The Ukraine delegation's presence in Washington, Rubio's impasse assessment, the Kyiv Independent's reporting that Moscow conditioned an energy truce on sanctions easing — all suggest the diesel arrangement may be one move in a developing endgame diplomacy. On this reading, the eighteen months is a negotiating window: sanctions relief as the currency with which Washington purchases Russian cooperation on Ukraine, with the renewal date as the enforcement mechanism. The risk, on this reading, is that the currency is spent before the purchase is made — relief now, concessions later, maybe never.

The newsroom's judgment: reading two best fits the documentary evidence — the duration, the absence of a cap, the unilateral process all point past emergency management. But reading three cannot be dismissed given the diplomatic context; if the license is tied to Ukraine endgame talks, its meaning changes substantially. What is analytically solid: whatever the intent, the structural effects (Moscow's renewal leverage, the transatlantic split, the precedent for sanctions partners) will obtain regardless of which narrative the administration prefers.

The missing piece

The largest unknown is whether the license is connected to Ukraine negotiations. The Kyiv Independent's reporting — Moscow conditioning an energy truce on sanctions easing — plus the Ukrainian delegation's Washington visit and Rubio's impasse remark, form a suggestive cluster. But no official has confirmed linkage, and the administration presents the license as pure fuel-price policy. If linkage exists, the license is diplomacy; if not, it is unilateral sanctions relief. The distinction determines how history judges it, and it is currently unresolvable from public sources.

The second gap is European follow-through. Brussels says it will not ease — but the EEAS listings proposal (827 entities, 744 individuals) is still pending the October 12 Council decision, and the practical question of enforcement divergence (American diesel markets open to Russia, European closed) has no precedent. Arbitrage, transshipment, and documentation fraud are the predictable consequences; whether the EU builds the enforcement to match its rhetoric is unknown.

The third gap is volumes. The tranche structure — up to 3 million tonnes a month tied to refinery conditions — assumes Russian refining capacity that Ukrainian drones are actively degrading. The license authorizes trade; it does not conjure diesel. Actual cargo flows, when tanker tracking confirms them, will be the empirical test of every claim made about this arrangement.

What to watch

First, the October 12 EU Foreign Affairs Council — the listings decision and any formal response to the American carve-out. Second, first cargoes: tanker tracking will confirm whether Russian diesel actually moves under the license and in what volumes. Third, the renewal politics: watch for the formation of a domestic pro-renewal constituency as April 2027 approaches. Fourth, any confirmed linkage to Ukraine endgame diplomacy — the missing piece. Fifth, congressional action: bipartisan criticism exists, but legislation has not materialized. Sixth, India's response — New Delhi's next move will signal how the sanctions-partner world prices American inconsistency. Seventh, the diesel price itself: if retail prices fall, the administration claims vindication; if they don't, Poroshenko's arithmetic gets its empirical test.

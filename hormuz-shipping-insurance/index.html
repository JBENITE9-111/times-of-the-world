The $20 Million Voyage: How the Strait of Hormuz Became the World's Most Expensive Waterway

War-risk insurance now costs up to a tenth of a supertanker's hull value per voyage. Tanker rates hit $1.3 million a day. Captains are offered six-figure bonuses to sail. This is what a maritime war does to the global economy.

The signal

The Strait of Hormuz is twenty-one miles wide at its narrowest point. Roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne petroleum liquids and about a fifth of its liquefied natural gas normally pass through it. For seven months — since the US-Israeli war on Iran began on February 28, 2026 — that waterway has been the site of a grinding maritime pressure campaign. In the last two weeks, the campaign intensified sharply: attacks on tankers hit their highest weekly level of the war, traffic through the strait fell to a two-month low, and the price of moving oil through the world's most important energy artery reached levels without precedent.

The numbers, drawn from maritime analytics firms, insurance brokers, and price reporting agencies via the Financial Times, Reuters, and industry press, describe a market undergoing violent repricing. War-risk insurance for Gulf voyages is running at 6 to 10 percent of a vessel's hull value — for a very large crude carrier, that translates to as much as $20 million in insurance for a single voyage. Tanker rates for Hormuz transits hit a record $1.3 million per day, at least twenty-six times the $20,000 to $50,000 per day those routes commanded last year. Captains are being offered bonuses above $100,000 simply to make the crossing. Bunker fuel at Fujairah, the nearest refueling port, costs $686 per tonne — 67 percent higher than a year ago. And the human cost is mounting: the International Maritime Organization counts at least 93 ships hit since February 28, with 24 sailors killed.

This paper examines how the maritime war works as an economic instrument — who pays, who profits, who absorbs the risk, and what the repricing reveals about the war's trajectory. The central argument: the Strait of Hormuz has not been closed, but it has been transformed into a two-tier waterway where only the well-insured, the well-paid, or the desperate transit — and the costs are being transmitted, tanker by tanker, into the diesel prices now dominating American politics.

What we can verify

The insurance shock. Insurance brokers cited by the Financial Times report that war-risk cover for vessels operating in the Gulf is running at 6 to 10 percent of hull value. For a VLCC — a very large crude carrier worth roughly $100-120 million — a single Gulf voyage can therefore carry up to $20 million in war-risk insurance alone. To put that in perspective: before the war, war-risk premiums for the region were a marginal line item, fractions of a percent. The current pricing represents an increase of one to two orders of magnitude. This is VERIFIED as reported broker data via the FT; the exact per-vessel figures vary, but the range is consistently reported across multiple outlets (Economic Times, TradersUnion, Chosun Biz, all citing the FT's broker sources).

The freight explosion. Tanker rates for Hormuz transits reached a record $1.3 million per day in the week of October 5-9, according to the FT's reporting. The comparable figure last year was roughly $20,000 to $50,000 per day — meaning rates have multiplied by at least twenty-six times. Eagle Intelligence's October 9 brief put VLCC rates for Gulf of Oman loadings above $1.2 million per day, consistent with the FT figure. Separately, only a limited number of ships are willing to make the trip at all, with cargoes being transferred to other tankers waiting off Fujairah in the Gulf of Oman — ship-to-ship transfers that add cost, time, and their own risks.

The traffic collapse. Multiple independent analytics firms document falling transits. Windward estimated 13 vessels crossed the strait on October 4, down from 24 on the same day a week earlier — a near-halving in seven days. Windward also estimated that about 2 percent of vessels transiting Hormuz in the third quarter were hit. Navilink Global, citing its own tracking, reported just 1 transit on September 27 against a pre-crisis baseline of 85 per day — a near-complete standstill on that day, though single-day figures are volatile and the weekly picture is less extreme. Eagle Intelligence reported crossings at a two-month low in the week of October 5-9. Kpler data indicates oil flows through Hormuz remained about one-third below prewar levels, even as overall Gulf oil flows (including pipeline and alternative routes) recovered to roughly 80-81 percent of pre-war levels.

The attack tempo. The International Maritime Organization has recorded at least 93 ships hit since February 28, with 24 sailors killed. The maritime security firm Vanguard counts at least 14 attacks in the Hormuz area since September 20, including four vessels struck in a single weekend in early October. Reuters-cited maritime security data recorded at least 13 tanker incidents — attacks, attempted attacks, drone overflights, VHF hails — around the strait between September 28 and October 5, the highest weekly total since the war began; the IMO's own count for the same week was nine. Nearly 20 commercial ships, mostly tankers, came under attack over the past month in Hormuz, the Persian Gulf, or waters off Oman. One specific incident: the Panama-flagged tanker MT On Peace, where eleven Indian crew members were injured. The US Navy-led Joint Maritime Information Center maintains the regional threat level at SEVERE.

The human price. Beyond the hardware economics, the labor market for seafarers has been transformed. The FT reports that tanker captains are being offered bonuses above $100,000 plus additional payments to cross the strait. Crews — drawn heavily from the Philippines, India, and Indonesia, plus Russia, Ukraine, and Eastern Europe — face what one seafarer representative described to the FT as an impossible choice between their jobs and their lives; the industry, the FT reported, effectively regards Hormuz crews in mercenary terms now. Many vessels transit at night with GPS/AIS signalling switched off. Twenty-four sailors dead since February is the grimmest number in this entire dataset.

The American military posture. The United States has deployed approximately 2,000 additional Marines to the Middle East, with senior officials meeting at Camp David on October 4. The US Navy has deployed defensive air capabilities to cover a route close to the Omani coast. But — and this is significant — the Navy continues to refuse shipping industry requests for military escorts through the strait itself, citing excessive risk. Washington will provide air cover near Oman; it will not convoy tankers through Hormuz.

Iran's stated position. Tehran says the strait will not reopen until its seven conditions under what is called the Islamabad Memorandum are met. Iranian lawmakers are advancing legislation to declare full Iranian control over the strait — a move the Trump administration has rejected. These are ATTRIBUTED claims about Iranian positions, reported via industry press; the memorandum's actual text and the legislation's status are not independently established in this newsroom's sourcing.

The sanctions overlay. On October 9, the United States sanctioned 22 tankers in its latest tranche against Iran's shadow fleet, according to Eagle Intelligence. This runs parallel to the physical attacks: while projectiles threaten hulls, sanctions threaten the ownership structures behind them.

Status notes: the insurance and freight figures are STRONGLY SUPPORTED (broker and market data via FT, consistently reported). Traffic and incident counts are STRONGLY SUPPORTED (multiple analytics firms, IMO). The "near-standstill" single-day figure is ATTRIBUTED to Navilink's tracking (volatile daily data). Iranian political claims are ATTRIBUTED. The 80-81 percent export recovery figure is ATTRIBUTED to Kpler and described even by its publisher as partly unverified.

What is being claimed

The "near-standstill" claim. Navilink Global reported that Hormuz traffic came to a near-complete standstill, with 1 transit on September 27 versus a 85-per-day baseline. This is a real data point from a real tracking firm, but single-day figures in a war zone are extremely volatile — vessels bunch, delay, and reroute — and the weekly data from Windward and Eagle shows severe reduction rather than literal standstill. The responsible reading: traffic has collapsed to a fraction of normal, with near-zero days occurring. Note also that Navilink's article discloses AI assistance in its production; it is an industry blog, not a primary source, and its figures should be cross-checked against Windward, Kpler, and the IMO — which, on the directional claim, they are.

Iran's seven conditions. The claim that Tehran will keep the strait restricted until seven conditions under the Islamabad Memorandum are met comes via industry reporting of Iranian statements. The memorandum itself, its signatories, and whether "seven conditions" is an accurate characterization are not established in open sourcing available to this newsroom. Treat as an ATTRIBUTED Iranian position, not as a documented diplomatic text.

The 80 percent recovery. Kpler's estimate that overall Gulf oil flows recovered to roughly 80-81 percent of pre-war levels — via pipelines, ship-to-ship transfers, and alternative routes — is widely cited, including by the EIA. But Kpler's own caveats and the "unverified figure" flag in industry reporting mean this should be read as an informed estimate, not a measured fact. The direction (partial recovery via workarounds) is STRONGLY SUPPORTED; the precise percentage is ATTRIBUTED.

Crew-bonus and "mercenary" framing. The $100,000-plus captain bonuses and the FT's characterization of Hormuz crews are reported via the FT's industry sources. They are plausible and consistent with the insurance math — when the hull premium is $20 million, a $100,000 bonus is rounding — but they remain single-outlet reported figures.

The strategic picture

The maritime campaign in Hormuz is best understood as taxation by violence. Iran cannot close the strait outright — doing so would strangle its own oil exports, which transit the same water, and would likely trigger the direct American military response Tehran has spent seven months trying to avoid. What it can do, and is doing, is raise the cost of transit to levels that function as a slow blockade: not by sinking every tanker, but by making every tanker voyage a financial and human gamble. Two percent of Q3 transits hit does not sound catastrophic until you translate it: a 1-in-50 chance of attack per transit, $20 million insurance tickets, $1.3 million daily rates, six-figure danger bonuses. At those prices, the market rations itself. Only the most valuable cargoes, the most risk-tolerant owners, or the most desperate charterers sail. Everyone else waits, reroutes, or pays the shuttle-trade premium off Fujairah.

This is why the insurance market is the true barometer of the war, more honest than any official statement. Insurers have no ideology; they have actuarial tables. When war-risk cover hits 10 percent of hull value, the underwriters are telling you, in the only language that matters, their assessed probability of loss. No Iranian press release and no Pentagon briefing conveys as much information as that premium. And the premium is screaming.

The adaptation economy that has grown around the strait deserves careful attention, because it determines how long the pressure can be sustained. Three workarounds are now structural. First, pipelines: Saudi Arabia restarted shipments through its East-West Pipeline to the Red Sea, bypassing Hormuz entirely; the EIA credits this and similar measures with restoring Gulf flows (excluding Iran) to over 81 percent of pre-war levels in September. The reported attacks on that very pipeline underscore that the workaround is itself a target. Second, ship-to-ship transfers off Fujairah and in the Gulf of Oman, where cargoes are shuttled from shuttle tankers to ocean-going vessels outside the strait — a trade the industry press notes continues despite explicitly elevated risk. Third, dark transits: tankers disabling AIS tracking and running at night, a practice the EIA itself documents. Each workaround adds cost, and each is a degradation of the safety and transparency systems the industry spent decades building. The dark transits in particular are a quiet scandal: the world's most important oil artery is now routinely navigated blind.

The American posture — air cover near Oman, no escorts through the strait, 2,000 more Marines, a Camp David meeting — reveals the limits of US power in this specific geometry. Escorting tankers through a strait where the adversary holds both shores and has demonstrated anti-ship capability is, as the Navy's refusal acknowledges, excessively risky. The United States can punish Iran — seven months of airstrikes attest to that — but it cannot cheaply secure the waterway. That asymmetry is the entire Iranian strategy: impose costs Washington cannot easily suppress, in a domain where American military superiority translates poorly into commercial security.

There is a further dimension that connects this paper to the diesel story. The EIA's October Short-Term Energy Outlook raised its Brent forecast to about $98 a barrel for 2026 (up 8 percent), sees $105 in the fourth quarter, and expects US retail diesel above $6 a gallon through October — explicitly citing disrupted Hormuz flows and tight diesel markets. Every $20 million insurance ticket and every $1.3 million daily rate feeds, through refinery margins and freight costs, into the diesel price that now dominates American electoral politics. The maritime war and the midterm calendar are not separate stories; they are the same story told in different units.

Finally, the human ledger. Twenty-four sailors dead. Eleven Indian crew injured on a single tanker. Crews from some of the world's poorest communities sailing dark, at night, through a shooting gallery, because the wages are higher than anything at home and the alternative is unemployment. The FT's mercenary framing is harsh, but the economics are stark: the industry has priced the risk of death into the wage, just as it priced the risk of hull loss into the premium. When historians write the economic history of this war, the insurance tables and the danger bonuses will tell them more than the communiqués.

The longer history: forty years of threatening the strait

Iran has been threatening the Strait of Hormuz for longer than most of today's tanker captains have been alive, and the historical record is essential for calibrating the current crisis. During the Iran-Iraq war of the 1980s, the so-called Tanker War saw both belligerents attack neutral commercial shipping in the Gulf; the United States responded with Operation Earnest Will, reflagging Kuwaiti tankers and escorting them — the last time Washington actually ran convoys through these waters, and the historical precedent that makes today's escort refusal so telling. In 2019, amid the US-Iran tensions of Trump's first term, a series of mysterious tanker attacks in the Gulf of Oman (attributed by Washington to Iran, denied by Tehran), the IRGC's seizure of the British-flagged Stena Impero, and the downing of an American drone brought the region to the edge of open conflict. Each episode followed the same script: Iranian pressure on shipping, American military signaling, and a negotiated or fizzled de-escalation.

What distinguishes 2026 from every previous episode is duration and context. The strait has now been a contested battlespace for over seven months — not a crisis measured in weeks but a sustained maritime war. Previous threats were coercive bargaining conducted in peacetime; the current campaign is being conducted inside an actual war, with Iran under sustained American and Israeli bombardment. That changes the logic fundamentally. In 2019, Iran used shipping pressure to signal resolve and extract sanctions relief. In 2026, with its territory being struck, the strait is both a retaliatory instrument and one of the few domains where Iran can impose costs that Washington cannot easily suppress — as the Navy's escort refusal concedes.

The legal dimension deserves attention because it will shape whatever settlement eventually comes. Under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, the Strait of Hormuz is subject to the regime of transit passage — ships of all states enjoy the right of continuous and expeditious transit, and bordering states may not hamper or suspend it. Iran signed but never ratified UNCLOS, and has periodically asserted expansive claims over the strait, including the argument that its security legislation gives it regulatory authority beyond what transit passage permits. The reported Iranian legislation to declare full control over the strait, if enacted, would be a direct challenge to the transit-passage regime — and the Trump administration's rejection of it signals that Washington views the strait as an international waterway whose closure would be an act of war, not an exercise of sovereignty. This legal contest matters because it defines the endgame: any durable settlement must resolve not just who shoots at whom, but under what legal regime the world's most important oil artery operates. The "seven conditions" attributed to Tehran suggest it understands this perfectly — the strait is leverage precisely because everyone agrees it must eventually be governed by rules, and Iran is auctioning its cooperation.

There is also the question the history forces: why has Iran never fully closed the strait, even now? Part of the answer is self-interest — Iranian oil exports transit the same water, and full closure would be economic suicide. Part is deterrence — Washington has made clear, across administrations, that closure would trigger a massive military response aimed at reopening it by force. And part is that partial closure is more useful than total closure: a strait that is dangerous but navigable generates a permanent uncertainty premium — in insurance, in freight, in oil prices — that a closed strait would convert into a finite military confrontation. The taxation-by-violence model described above is not a failure to close the strait; it is the optimal strategy for a power that wants leverage without suicide. Understanding that is essential for forecasting: the pressure will likely persist at calibrated levels until the diplomatic price is right, not escalate to the closure that everyone fears and no one quite expects.

The competing narratives

Reading one: contained risk, functioning market. On this reading, the system is working as designed. Prices are doing their job: extreme risk commands extreme premiums, traffic self-rations, workarounds (pipelines, STS transfers, dark transits) restore four-fifths of flows, and no systemic supply crisis has materialized. The 2 percent hit rate, while serious, means 98 percent of transits complete. Insurers have not withdrawn cover — they have repriced it, which is what insurers do. The market is stressed, not broken.

Reading two: slow strangulation. On this reading, the headline flow figures mask a deteriorating trend. Attacks are at record weekly highs and rising, not falling. The Navy refuses escorts. Iran is legislating permanent control. Each workaround is itself being targeted (the East-West Pipeline attacks) or degraded (dark transits eroding safety). The $1.3 million daily rate is not an equilibrium — it is a rationing price that will climb further if the attack tempo continues. The EIA's own forecast ($105 Brent in Q4) assumes continued constraint. On this reading, the market has not adapted to the risk; it is being consumed by it, one premium hike at a time.

Reading three: the negotiated waterway. A third reading sees the maritime campaign as fundamentally coercive rather than destructive — calibrated to maximize negotiating leverage rather than to close the strait. The seven conditions, the legislation theater, the calibrated tempo: all signal that Tehran views the strait as a bargaining chip for the post-election settlement, not as a battlefield to be won. On this reading, the insurance spike is the point — every dollar of premium is a dollar of pressure on Washington — and the campaign's intensity will track the diplomatic calendar, easing if negotiations advance and spiking if they stall.

The newsroom's judgment: readings one and two are both substantially true and describe different time horizons — the market is coping now (reading one) while the trend lines point toward unsustainability (reading two). The record attack tempo, the Navy's escort refusal, and Iran's legislative moves all weigh against complacency. Reading three is plausible as a description of Iranian intent but the evidence is circumstantial; Tehran's actual decision calculus is opaque, and the tempo may reflect operational momentum as much as diplomatic calibration.

The missing piece

The central unknown is the actual Iranian command picture: which attacks are IRGC-directed, which are proxy or opportunistic, and which reported incidents are exaggerated or fabricated. The NV Sunshine contradiction — IRGC claim versus owner denial — shows that not every announced strike is a real strike, which means the incident counts themselves contain an unknown fraction of noise. Until there is independent attribution for the major incidents, the entire analytical edifice rests on counts whose composition is uncertain.

The second gap is the insurance market's next move. Brokers have repriced; the question is whether underwriters begin withdrawing cover entirely for Hormuz transits, as opposed to pricing it prohibitively. Cover withdrawal would be a phase change — from expensive to unavailable — and there is no public data yet on whether it is being contemplated. The Eagle Intelligence note that "cover withdrawal remains unconfirmed in any source" is the honest state of play.

The third gap is crew data. Twenty-four dead is an IMO figure, but injury counts, the nationality breakdown, and the conditions under which crews are consenting to these voyages are poorly documented. The human story of this war is being told in fragments.

Insurance pricing data — actual premium schedules rather than broker ranges — remains awaited.

What to watch

First, underwriter behavior: repricing versus cover withdrawal — the phase-change question. Second, the attack tempo: whether the record weekly high extends or breaks. Third, traffic data: Windward and Kpler weekly transits as the real-time barometer. Fourth, the Iranian legislation on strait control and any American response to it. Fifth, the shadow-fleet sanctions' practical effect on the STS shuttle trade. Sixth, crew casualty and injury reporting — the human ledger. Seventh, the diesel price transmission: EIA weekly data will show whether the maritime premium is flowing through to American pumps ahead of November 3.
